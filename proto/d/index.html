mk is a command-line toolkit for making music videos with MMD models in Blender. A project is one mk.toml: mk builds the scene from it (sets, props, vehicles, characters, poses, grips, performance, secondary motion, lights, shots, cut effects, type in the world and over the picture), measures it with checks, renders every output and grades the result into MP4s with the song. Research01

You can read a voice model's vocal range straight off its weights

Source · pop-maker-studio ↗

When you use an AI voice model to sound like someone else, there's one knob you always end up fighting: pitch. If the model was trained on a deep voice and yours is high, you have to guess. Shift down an octave? Convert, listen, try again. Everyone does it by ear, and it's the single most common "why does this sound bad."

You don't have to guess. The model already knows its own range. It's sitting in the weights, and you can read it out in a microsecond, without playing a note.

Why the range is in there

These models (RVC) handle pitch with a small lookup table called a pitch embedding: 256 slots, one per pitch step from low to high, each holding a learned vector. A slot only changes during training when the training audio actually hits that pitch. If the voice never sang low, the low slots never get touched.

And every voice model is fine-tuned from the same shared starting point (a "base" model). So line up a finished voice's pitch table against that base, look at which slots moved, and you've got a map of exactly which pitches the voice was trained on. That map is its vocal range.

How you read it (it's basically subtraction)

The code is short. At export time (rvc_onnx.cpp:1359-1410):

  1. Subtract the base pitch table from the voice's, slot by slot.
  2. For each slot, measure how far it moved (the length of that difference).
  3. Smooth it a touch, then keep the slots that moved more than ~30% of the biggest mover.

That's the range: a 256-slot "trained / not trained" map.

Auto-key is then just arithmetic (vc_onnx.cpp:316-381). The input's pitch over time is already a list of numbers, and you measure it once anyway as a normal first step. Shifting it an octave is multiplying those numbers; checking "unshifted vs. up vs. down" is counting how many of them fall inside the trained map. You pick the shift with the best count, and ties stay put. Nothing is converted or played to decide. The choice happens on the pitch numbers, before any audio is generated.

The payoff: you sing in your natural pitch and it lands you in the model's range automatically. A high voice into a model trained on a deep one just works, with no knob.

The part that isn't about audio

Nothing here is specific to voices. Any model with an embedding lookup, like the word table in a language model or the class table in a classifier, has the same property: a row only moves if training touched it. Diff a fine-tuned model's embedding against its base, and the rows that moved tell you what the fine-tune actually saw. Vocal range is just the case where that map happens to be immediately useful, and where you can turn it into a feature people press a button on.

Related work

Other people read things out of weight differences too, for different goals: some train a model to describe how a fine-tune changed its behavior; others reconstruct training examples from gradients. This is the simple, narrow end of that street. It reads one concrete number (a range) straight off the embedding, with subtraction and a threshold, and uses it to fix a real annoyance.

Research02

Training and serving real LLMs inside 12 GB of VRAM

One RX 7700 XT (12.87 GB VRAM), 32 GB of DDR4, and a wall-clock obsession. Everything below was measured on that machine — training runs, serving tiers, and the crashes in between — with two questions: how far can you train your own model, and what is the biggest uncensored model you can actually live with?

256k-context training from scratch at 41k tok/s, a 1M-token training step that fits, and a 35B-parameter MoE serving uncensored — all inside a card that costs less than a game.

Training from scratch: the 256k trainer

The trainer is fully from-scratch PyTorch on ROCm (torch 2.9, HSA override 11.0.0). At 84.5M parameters it sustains 41,830 tok/s at 32k context with a 6.9 GB peak, and the full 256k window trains at 41 s/step. The machinery that unlocks long context on a small card: query-chunked flex attention with per-chunk backward (no full-block graph), a shared global mask cache, FP8 e4m3 CPU-offloaded boundary tensors, a kv pre-pass with a single detached backward, norm offload, CPU-parked chunk assembly, Muon per-head plus AdamW, QK-norm, and chunked cross-entropy. The 1M-token training step works too — 183 s/step at 5.7k tok/s with a 9.8 GB peak — via boundary quantize-offload and careful host/device placement.

The shape law nobody told us

At this scale, wide and shallow beats deep — and the quality guard (40-step validation on real traces) caught it before any speed claim could hide it. At 84.5M, 4 layers × d768 × d_ff3072 beat both 3L and 6L at the 100-step horizon (val 2.628). At 306M, 6 layers × d1536 with d_ff = 4×d (6144) beat the deeper 8L shape at every horizon and ran 20% faster (8,733 tok/s, 5.3 GB peak, val 2.9079 at 40 steps). The rule of thumb that fell out: layers ≈ d/256 at ~350M scale, ≈ d/192 at ~85M. Learning rate must be re-swept per scale — 1e-3 at 84.5M, 4.5e-4 at 306M. Everything is bit-identical reproducible (fixed seeds, synthetic data, deterministic harness).

Serving: the 100 tok/s physics problem

Getting 100 tok/s decode with a Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on 12 GB is a VRAM-budget problem, and the budget wins. Measured tiers: the 2B Q4 hits 140 tok/s short-context; the 35B at IQ2 hits 73 tok/s at 64k with KV on GPU; the same model serves 262k context at 29 tok/s only by offloading KV to RAM (the whole-KV-per-token readback is what costs the speed); MTP speculative decoding recovers ~82 tok/s at short context; and the uncensored HauhauCS IQ2_M variant runs the whole stack. The pattern across every crash we survived: a compositor needs ~1 GB of VRAM, and below ~0.6 GB of headroom the amdgpu driver wedges hard — Xorg and KDE both died with core dumps before we found the working envelope (CPU-parked experts, KV in RAM, software-GL terminals).

The 2027 MoE landscape, from inside 12 GB

Surveying what actually competes: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is the class leader on coding and speed; Gemma 4 26B A4B leads general knowledge (57.1 vs 50.5 public score) and fits the same envelope at Q3; Qwen3.6-27B is dense (better quality, 4× slower, won't fit at usable quant); PrismML's Bonsai ternary is the only 12GB path to the 27B and remains unproven. Uncensored options are real: TrevorJS's Gemma 4 abliterations (0/100 refusals) and the HauhauCS Qwen3.6 variant both run here. The honest summary: on 12 GB you pick speed + coding (Qwen 35B) or general quality + writing (Gemma 26B), and Sonnet 4.5 keeps the coding crown only if you're willing to pay per token and accept its alignment.
